Maximum likelihood estimation uses only the data. When the data are few, the estimate can be extreme in a way that nobody would accept: a coin that lands heads in each of five tosses has maximum likelihood estimate $$\hat\theta = 1$$ for its head probability, which asserts that tails is impossible. Most people would instead report a value somewhat below one, because they believe before seeing any data that coins are roughly fair. Maximum a posteriori (MAP) estimation formalizes this by maximizing the posterior density instead of the likelihood. Below I set up the estimator, work out the coin example with a Beta prior, and point out what the MAP estimate is and is not.

## From likelihood to posterior

Let $$x = (x_1, \dots, x_n)$$ be data with density $$p(x \mid \theta)$$ for a parameter $$\theta$$ in an open set $$\Theta \subseteq \R^p$$. The maximum likelihood estimate maximizes $$\theta \mapsto p(x \mid \theta)$$. In the Bayesian approach, $$\theta$$ is given a prior density $$\pi(\theta)$$ with respect to Lebesgue measure on $$\Theta$$, and Bayes' theorem gives the posterior density

$$
\pi(\theta \mid x) = \frac{p(x \mid \theta)\, \pi(\theta)}{m(x)},
\qquad
m(x) = \int_\Theta p(x \mid \vartheta)\, \pi(\vartheta)\, \dd\vartheta ,
$$

provided the marginal density $$m(x)$$ is finite and positive. The **MAP estimate** is a maximizer of the posterior density,

$$
\hat\theta_{\text{MAP}} \in \argmax_{\theta \in \Theta}\, \pi(\theta \mid x),
$$

assuming a maximizer exists. Since $$m(x)$$ does not depend on $$\theta$$ and the logarithm is increasing, it is equivalently a maximizer of $$\log p(x \mid \theta) + \log \pi(\theta)$$, and the intractable integral $$m(x)$$ is never needed. For i.i.d. observations with density $$f(\cdot \mid \theta)$$,

$$
\hat\theta_{\text{MAP}} \in \argmax_{\theta \in \Theta}\, \Bigl\{ \sum_{i=1}^n \log f(x_i \mid \theta) + \log \pi(\theta) \Bigr\}.
$$

Compared with maximum likelihood, the only change is the additive term $$\log \pi(\theta)$$. It acts as a penalty that pulls the estimate towards regions the prior favours. Many regularized estimators are MAP estimates in disguise: in linear regression with Gaussian errors, a prior $$\beta \sim N(0, \tau^2 I)$$ gives ridge regression and independent Laplace priors on the coefficients give the lasso. The sum of $$n$$ log-likelihood terms grows with $$n$$ while the log prior does not, so for regular models and a prior density that is positive and continuous at the truth, the influence of the prior fades as data accumulate. With few data it can matter a great deal, which is exactly the situation where it is useful.

## The coin

Let $$x_1, \dots, x_n$$ be independent Bernoulli($$\theta$$) variables, with $$x_i = 1$$ for heads. Write $$n_1 = \sum_i x_i$$ for the number of heads and $$n_2 = n - n_1$$ for the number of tails. The likelihood is

$$
p(x \mid \theta) = \prod_{i=1}^n \theta^{x_i} (1-\theta)^{1 - x_i} = \theta^{n_1} (1-\theta)^{n_2}.
$$

(If only the count $$n_1$$ is recorded, its binomial probability has an extra factor $$\binom{n}{n_1}$$, which does not depend on $$\theta$$ and changes nothing below.) For $$0 < n_1 < n$$, the log-likelihood $$n_1 \log\theta + n_2\log(1-\theta)$$ is strictly concave on $$(0,1)$$ with derivative $$n_1/\theta - n_2/(1-\theta)$$, which vanishes at $$\hat\theta_{\text{MLE}} = n_1/n$$. If $$n_1 = n$$ the likelihood $$\theta^n$$ is increasing and the maximum over $$[0,1]$$ is at $$1 = n_1/n$$ again. Five heads in five tosses gives $$\hat\theta_{\text{MLE}} = 1$$.

**The Beta prior.** A prior that encodes "the coin is probably close to fair, and heads and tails are treated alike" should be symmetric about $$1/2$$ with its mode there. The Beta family

$$
\pi(\theta) = \frac{\theta^{\alpha-1} (1-\theta)^{\beta-1}}{B(\alpha, \beta)}, \qquad 0 < \theta < 1,
\qquad
B(\alpha, \beta) = \int_0^1 t^{\alpha - 1} (1-t)^{\beta - 1}\, \dd t = \frac{\Gamma(\alpha)\Gamma(\beta)}{\Gamma(\alpha+\beta)},
$$

with $$\alpha, \beta > 0$$ and $$\Gamma(s) = \int_0^\infty t^{s-1} e^{-t}\, \dd t$$, contains such priors. Its mean and variance are

$$
\E\theta = \frac{\alpha}{\alpha+\beta},
\qquad
\Var\theta = \frac{\alpha\beta}{(\alpha+\beta)^2(\alpha+\beta+1)},
$$

and when $$\alpha, \beta > 1$$ the density is unimodal with mode $$(\alpha-1)/(\alpha+\beta-2)$$, by the same calculation as for the likelihood above. A symmetric prior $$\alpha = \beta$$ has mean $$1/2$$ and variance $$1/\{4(2\alpha+1)\}$$, so larger $$\alpha$$ expresses a firmer belief that the coin is fair. The choice $$\alpha = \beta = 1$$ is the uniform distribution, and $$\alpha = \beta < 1$$ gives U-shaped densities that favour strongly biased coins.

**The posterior.** Multiplying likelihood and prior,

$$
\pi(\theta \mid x) \propto \theta^{n_1} (1-\theta)^{n_2} \cdot \theta^{\alpha-1}(1-\theta)^{\beta-1} = \theta^{n_1 + \alpha - 1} (1-\theta)^{n_2 + \beta - 1},
$$

which is the kernel of a Beta distribution. Since a density is determined by its kernel up to the normalizing constant,

$$
\theta \mid x \sim \mathrm{Beta}(n_1 + \alpha,\; n_2 + \beta).
$$

The prior and the posterior belong to the same family: the Beta family is **conjugate** to the Bernoulli likelihood. The update has a simple reading: $$\alpha$$ and $$\beta$$ act as pseudo-counts of heads and tails observed before the experiment, and the data add $$n_1$$ and $$n_2$$ to them.

<div class="result" markdown="1">
**Proposition 1.** Let $$a = n_1 + \alpha - 1$$ and $$b = n_2 + \beta - 1$$, and assume $$a \ge 0$$, $$b \ge 0$$ and $$a + b > 0$$. Then the posterior density has the unique maximizer

$$
\hat\theta_{\text{MAP}} = \frac{n_1 + \alpha - 1}{n + \alpha + \beta - 2}
$$

over $$[0, 1]$$, where the density is extended to the endpoints by continuity.
</div>

<div class="proof" markdown="1">
*Proof.* Up to a positive constant the posterior density is $$g(\theta) = \theta^{a}(1-\theta)^{b}$$. If $$a, b > 0$$, then $$g$$ vanishes at both endpoints and is positive inside, and $$\log g$$ is strictly concave on $$(0,1)$$ with derivative

$$
\frac{a}{\theta} - \frac{b}{1-\theta},
$$

which is zero exactly at $$\theta = a/(a+b)$$. If $$b = 0 < a$$, then $$g(\theta) = \theta^a$$ is strictly increasing and the maximizer is $$1 = a/(a+b)$$; the case $$a = 0 < b$$ is symmetric.
</div>

The hypotheses matter. If $$a < 0$$ or $$b < 0$$, possible when $$\alpha < 1$$ or $$\beta < 1$$, the posterior density is unbounded near an endpoint and has no maximizer. If $$a = b = 0$$, which happens for the uniform prior with no data, the posterior is uniform and every point is a maximizer.

## Reading the formula

The MAP estimate is a weighted average of the sample proportion and the prior mode. When $$\alpha, \beta > 1$$ and $$n \ge 1$$,

$$
\hat\theta_{\text{MAP}} = w\, \frac{n_1}{n} + (1-w)\, \frac{\alpha - 1}{\alpha + \beta - 2},
\qquad
w = \frac{n}{n + \alpha + \beta - 2},
$$

as one checks by multiplying out. The prior behaves like $$\alpha + \beta - 2$$ additional observations placed at the prior mode. Three special cases are worth recording.

- With no data ($$n = 0$$), the MAP estimate is the prior mode, as it should be.
- With the uniform prior $$\alpha = \beta = 1$$, the posterior density is proportional to the likelihood and $$\hat\theta_{\text{MAP}} = n_1/n = \hat\theta_{\text{MLE}}$$.
- With a fixed prior and $$n \to \infty$$, $$w \to 1$$ and $$\hat\theta_{\text{MAP}} - \hat\theta_{\text{MLE}} = O(1/n)$$; the prior's effect is of smaller order than the sampling error, which is of order $$n^{-1/2}$$.

For the five heads in five tosses and a symmetric prior $$\alpha = \beta$$, the MAP estimate is $$(\alpha + 4)/(2\alpha + 3)$$: it equals $$1$$ for $$\alpha = 1$$, $$6/7 \approx 0.86$$ for $$\alpha = 2$$, $$9/13 \approx 0.69$$ for $$\alpha = 5$$ and $$14/23 \approx 0.61$$ for $$\alpha = 10$$, and it tends to $$1/2$$ as $$\alpha \to \infty$$. Five observations carry little weight against a prior worth eighteen pseudo-observations.

## What the MAP estimate is not

**It is not invariant under reparameterization.** Maximum likelihood estimates are equivariant: if $$\phi = h(\theta)$$ for a bijection $$h$$, the MLE of $$\phi$$ is $$h(\hat\theta_{\text{MLE}})$$, because the likelihood is a function and reparameterizing only relabels its argument. A posterior density, by contrast, picks up a Jacobian. For the log-odds $$\phi = \log\{\theta/(1-\theta)\}$$, with inverse $$\theta = e^\phi/(1 + e^\phi)$$ and $$\dd\theta/\dd\phi = \theta(1-\theta)$$, the posterior density of $$\phi$$ is

$$
\pi_\phi(\phi \mid x) = \pi(\theta \mid x)\, \theta(1-\theta) \propto \theta^{n_1 + \alpha}(1-\theta)^{n_2 + \beta}, \qquad \theta = \frac{e^\phi}{1 + e^\phi}.
$$

Since $$\theta$$ is an increasing function of $$\phi$$, this is maximized at the $$\phi$$ corresponding to $$\theta = (n_1 + \alpha)/(n + \alpha + \beta)$$, which differs from the MAP estimate on the $$\theta$$ scale. With the uniform prior and five heads in five tosses, the MAP estimate is $$\theta = 1$$ on the probability scale and $$\phi = \log 6$$, that is $$\theta = 6/7$$, on the log-odds scale. The mode of a density is not a property of the distribution alone; it depends on the coordinates in which the density is written.

**It is not the posterior mean.** The posterior mean $$(n_1 + \alpha)/(n + \alpha + \beta)$$ minimizes the posterior expected squared error and is the usual Bayes estimate. It coincides with the log-odds MAP estimate above, which is special to this model, and it is never at the boundary: with the uniform prior and five heads it is $$6/7$$. The MAP estimate is attractive mainly computationally, because it requires optimization rather than integration, and for that reason it is widely used in high-dimensional models where the posterior cannot be summarized in any other way. It reports a single point and no uncertainty; the full posterior, here available in closed form, gives credible intervals as well.

The Beta–Bernoulli model and conjugate analysis are treated in detail in Gelman et al., *Bayesian Data Analysis*, and in Hoff, *A First Course in Bayesian Statistical Methods*.
